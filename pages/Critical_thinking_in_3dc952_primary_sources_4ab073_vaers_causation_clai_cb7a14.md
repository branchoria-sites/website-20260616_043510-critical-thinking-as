## Introduction

The Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS) is frequently cited in social media posts as proof that vaccines caused large numbers of injuries or deaths. The problem is that VAERS was never designed to prove causation. It is an early-warning reporting system that helps researchers detect possible safety signals that may deserve further investigation. A report in VAERS means that an event happened after vaccination and was reported; it does not mean the vaccine caused the event. Understanding this distinction is a useful exercise in [critical thinking]({{ 'critical-thinking-in-the-age-of-social/' | relative_url }}) because it shows how real public data can be transformed into misleading claims when its purpose and limitations are ignored. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdc.gov/vaccine-safety-systems/vaers/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdc.gov">[CDC+2vaers.hhs.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Establishing a causal relationship requires rigorous...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Why VAERS Accepts Unverified Reports

VAERS is jointly managed by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). It was created as a broad surveillance system intended to cast a wide net. Anyone can submit a report, including doctors, patients, family members, manufacturers and other observers. In some circumstances, healthcare providers and manufacturers are required to report adverse events even when they are unsure whether a vaccine played any role. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vaers.hhs.gov/reportevent.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vaers.hhs.gov">[vaers.hhs.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vaers.hhs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">VAER S</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report an Adverse Event - HHS.govVAERS accepts and analyzes reports of adverse events (AEs) after a person has received a vaccina...</span></span></span>

This design is intentional. A system that rejects reports because causation has not yet been established would miss potential warning signs. Instead, VAERS prioritises sensitivity over certainty. Reports can contain incomplete, inaccurate, coincidental or unverified information, but investigators would rather receive too many reports than overlook a genuine safety concern. wonder.cdc.gov+2Bloomberg School of Public Health <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://wonder.cdc.gov/wonder/help/vaers.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wonder.cdc.gov">[wonder.cdc.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wonder.cdc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cdc.gov.</span></span></span>

A useful analogy is a smoke alarm. A smoke alarm is meant to detect possible danger, not to determine the exact cause of a fire. When the alarm sounds, investigators still need to determine whether there is a real fire, what caused it, and how serious it is. VAERS functions in a similar way as a surveillance tool. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26209838/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Safety monitoring in the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting...by TT Shimabukuro · 2015 · Cited by 715 — Generally, VAERS data cannot...</span></span></span>

## The Difference Between Signal Detection and Causation

The most common misunderstanding about VAERS is the assumption that a report equals proof. In reality, health events happen every day in large populations regardless of vaccination status. Heart attacks, strokes, cancers, miscarriages and deaths occur naturally across millions of people. Some of these events will inevitably happen shortly after vaccination simply because of timing. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IN12486.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: everycrsreport.com">[Every CRS Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">everycrsreport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sometimes vaccination coincides with a subsequent...Read more...</span></span></span>

For this reason, CDC, FDA and VAERS documentation repeatedly state that reports alone cannot establish a causal relationship between a vaccine and an adverse event. Determining causation requires additional [evidence]({{ 'evidence/' | relative_url }}), including medical review, epidemiological studies, comparison with background rates, biological plausibility and analysis in other surveillance systems. ## How Raw Report Counts Become Misleading Charts

A common social-media tactic is to present a chart showing thousands of VAERS reports and then imply that every report represents a confirmed vaccine injury or death. This interpretation ignores how the database works. Official VAERS documentation explicitly warns that report totals alone cannot be used as evidence that a vaccine caused an event, nor as evidence of the frequency or severity of vaccine-related problems. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vaers.hhs.gov/data.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vaers.hhs.gov">[vaers.hhs.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vaers.hhs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">VAER S</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Data - HHS.gov8 May 2025 — The number of reports alone cannot be interpreted as evidence of a causal association between a vaccin...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2025</span></span></span>

## A Typical False Causation Claim

A recurring online claim states that VAERS contains a certain number of death reports and therefore proves vaccines killed that many people. This reasoning confuses temporal association with causation.

VAERS accepts reports of deaths occurring after vaccination because investigators need to know about them. Reporting requirements may encourage submission even when no causal link is suspected.
