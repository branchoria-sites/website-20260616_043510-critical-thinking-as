## Introduction

Professional fact-checkers do not begin by asking whether an entire post, speech, video or article is true. They begin by deciding exactly which claim deserves testing. This choice is one of the most important parts of the fact-checking process because [evidence]({{ 'evidence/' | relative_url }}) can only be applied fairly when the assertion being examined is clear, specific and relevant to the public. In an information environment shaped by social media algorithms, viral rumours and AI-generated content, many messages contain several claims at once. Fact-checkers therefore narrow the focus to a claim that is both checkable and important enough to justify investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fullfact.org/about/how-we-fact-check/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fullfact.org">[Full Fact]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fullfact.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Full FactHow we fact check – Full FactHow we choose what to check. ## Select the claim of public interest

The first question is not “Is this controversial?” but “Is this worth checking?”

Many fact-checking organisations explicitly prioritise claims that affect public understanding or could cause real-world harm if left unchallenged. Full Fact, for example, states that it checks claims in public debate that are of public interest and gives priority to claims with significant potential impact on people's lives. Claims may be selected because they are widely shared online, repeated by influential figures, or prominently featured in major media coverage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fullfact.org/about/how-we-fact-check/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fullfact.org">[Full Fact]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fullfact.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Full FactHow we fact check – Full FactHow we choose what to check. ### Why popularity alone is not enough

A viral post is not automatically a good fact-check candidate. Fact-checkers generally look for a combination of reach and factual significance.

For example, a post saying “This policy will destroy the economy” expresses a prediction and value judgement. A post saying “The policy increases taxes by 20% next month” contains a concrete factual assertion that can be tested against official documents. Professional fact-checkers are more likely to investigate the second statement because evidence can directly confirm or refute it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-10820/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commonslibrary.parliament.uk">[House of Commons Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commonslibrary.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">House of Commons Library How to check facts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">House of Commons LibraryHow to check facts - The House of Commons Library5 May 2026 — Fact-checking usually involves following a claim ba...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2026</span></span></span>

## Name assumptions hidden inside the wording

Once a claim has been selected, fact-checkers often discover that the apparent claim is not the real one.

Social media posts frequently compress several assumptions into a single sentence, image or caption. A photograph labelled “Taken yesterday outside a hospital” contains at least three factual elements:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The image is genuine.
* The location is a hospital.
* The date is yesterday.

</div>

Each component may require separate verification.

Professional fact-checking therefore involves extracting the central assertion and making implicit assumptions visible. Reuters notes that after identifying content to check, its team first identifies and summarises the key claims before investigating the foundations and origins of those claims.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/fact-check/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reuters ha establecido una unidad de fact-checking que revisa material visual y declaraciones en redes sociales. This process is especially important with AI-generated and edited media. An image may be authentic while its caption is false. A quote may be real while its context is distorted. A statistic may be accurate while the conclusion drawn from it is unsupported. Unless the exact claim is isolated, the fact-check risks answering a different question from the one audiences are actually asking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/fact-check/about/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reuters ha establecido una unidad de fact-checking que revisa material visual y declaraciones en redes sociales. ### Turning a post into a testable sentence

Professional fact-checkers often reformulate messy content into a claim that can stand on its own.

Research on claim extraction for fact-checking describes this as "decontextualisation": rewriting a claim so it remains understandable outside the original document or conversation. This helps fact-checkers retrieve evidence consistently and ensures readers know exactly what was tested.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03239" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Document-level Claim Extraction and Decontextualisation for Fact-Checking</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Document-level Claim Extraction and Decontextualisation for Fact-CheckingJune 5, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 5, 2024</span></span></span>

A post stating:

> “They've been hiding this from us for months.”

might be rewritten into a checkable claim such as:

> “Government agency X possessed document Y before date Z and did not publish it.”

The rewritten version exposes the factual proposition that evidence can address.

## Apply the same evidence standard to the claim

Claim selection is closely tied to fairness.

The International Fact-Checking Network (IFCN) Code of Principles requires participating organisations to use the same standard and process for every fact check and to let evidence determine conclusions rather than political preference. Similar commitments appear in the standards of multiple fact-checking organisations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org/the-commitments" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org">[IFCN Code of Principles+2FactCheckNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Signatory organizations fact-check claims using the same standard for every fact check. They do not concentrate...</span></span></span>

That principle begins before evidence gathering. If fact-checkers define claims differently depending on who made them, the process becomes inconsistent. ### Avoiding the "moving target" problem

A common difficulty in online debates is that claims shift during discussion. When evidence challenges one version, a speaker may retreat to a broader or vaguer version.

Professional fact-checkers reduce this problem by fixing the wording of the claim before beginning the assessment. Once the claim is defined, the evidence standard remains stable. The fact-check is no longer evaluating every implication, emotional reaction or political argument surrounding the topic. ## Why this step matters in the age of AI
