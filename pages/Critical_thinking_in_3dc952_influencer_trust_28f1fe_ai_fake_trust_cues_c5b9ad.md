## Introduction

AI can now imitate many of the signals people use to decide whether a messenger is trustworthy. A convincing accent, a familiar voice, a polished explainer, a believable local reference, or even a screenshot that appears to show insider information can all be generated or manipulated at scale. The result is not merely fake content. It is the appearance of credibility itself.


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Critical_thinking_in_3dc952_influencer_trust_28f1fe_ai_fake_trust_cues_c5b9ad-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="AI Impersonation illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Critical_thinking_in_3dc952_influencer_trust_28f1fe_ai_fake_trust_cues_c5b9ad-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Critical_thinking_in_3dc952_influencer_trust_28f1fe_ai_fake_trust_cues_c5b9ad-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
This matters because people rarely assess every claim from first principles. Instead, they rely on shortcuts: Does this person seem genuine? Do they sound knowledgeable? Do they appear connected to my community? AI systems can increasingly reproduce those cues, making fabricated messengers and fabricated claims look more persuasive than they would have been only a few years ago. Researchers have begun describing this problem as one of “synthetic credibility” or “synthetic trust”: the ability of AI-generated content to borrow the surface signals that normally help people judge reliability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03348" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SynCred-Bench: Benchmarking Synthetic Credibility in AI-Generated Visual MisinformationJune 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 2, 2026</span></span></span>

## Which Identity Cues AI Can Imitate

The most powerful feature of modern generative AI is not that it can create content. It is that it can create content that appears to come from a particular kind of person.

A fake messenger no longer has to be a generic anonymous account. AI can help construct an entire identity package that feels authentic to a specific audience.

Some of the cues AI can imitate include:

* **Voice and speech patterns.** Voice-cloning systems can reproduce tone, pacing, pronunciation and emotional style from relatively small audio samples. This allows scammers, propagandists and impersonators to sound like family members, creators, executives or public figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ftc.gov/policy/advocacy-research/tech-at-ftc/2023/11/preventing-harms-ai-enabled-voice-cloning" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ftc.gov">[Federal Trade Commission+2Federal Trade Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ftc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">preventing harms ai enabled voice cloning</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Trade CommissionPreventing the Harms of AI-enabled Voice Cloning16 Nov 2023 — The FTC is announcing the Voice Cloning Challenge t...</span></span></span>
* **Writing style.** Large language models can mimic the structure, vocabulary and mannerisms associated with particular communities, professions or personalities.
* **Visual identity.** AI-generated images and video can create faces that appear trustworthy, professional or relatable while depicting people who do not exist. Deepfake systems can also place real identities into fabricated situations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.21157" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unmasking Synthetic Realities in Generative AI: A Comprehensive Review of Adversarially Robust Deepfake Detection SystemsJuly 24, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 24, 2025</span></span></span>
* **Community belonging.** AI can incorporate local references, neighbourhood names, cultural jokes, sports teams, regional slang or shared concerns that signal “I am one of you”.
* **Apparent expertise.** Professionally formatted charts, diagrams, presentations and explainers can make a fabricated source appear knowledgeable even when the underlying information is weak or false.

The important point is that trust cues are often social rather than factual. People frequently judge credibility from signals surrounding a claim before evaluating the [evidence]({{ 'evidence/' | relative_url }}) itself. AI systems are becoming increasingly effective at reproducing those surrounding signals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04951" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

## Why Polish and Local Detail Can Mislead

For many years, obvious errors were a warning sign. Scam messages often contained poor grammar, awkward phrasing or unrealistic stories. Generative AI changes that dynamic.

Modern AI systems can produce fluent text, coherent explanations and persuasive narratives. They can also customise messages for particular audiences, creating the impression that a speaker understands local conditions, shared experiences or community concerns. This can make a message feel authentic even when the source is fabricated.

Researchers studying synthetic credibility have found that AI-generated misinformation can incorporate realistic layouts, convincing visual design and familiar credibility markers that make detection difficult for both people and technical systems. Human reviewers in one recent benchmark struggled to identify many examples of AI-generated misinformation built around these credibility cues.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03348" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SynCred-Bench: Benchmarking Synthetic Credibility in AI-Generated Visual MisinformationJune 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 2, 2026</span></span></span>

The persuasive effect often comes from accumulation. A single detail might not convince anyone. But a message that includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* a believable profile photograph,
* references to local events,
* emotionally appropriate language,
* apparent insider knowledge,
* and professional presentation,

</div>

can create a powerful impression that the messenger is genuine.

This is especially relevant in communities built around influencers and identity-based trust. Followers may already be accustomed to informal communication styles, personal stories and local references. AI can reproduce many of those same patterns, making it harder to distinguish genuine familiarity from manufactured familiarity.

## When a Familiar Voice Is Not Proof

Voice cloning illustrates the problem particularly clearly.

People often regard voices as strong evidence of identity. Hearing a familiar voice feels different from reading a message. Yet AI systems can now generate highly convincing synthetic speech from brief recordings. Regulators and consumer-protection agencies have repeatedly warned that voice-cloning technology is being used in fraud schemes involving relatives, executives and public officials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ftc.gov/policy/advocacy-research/tech-at-ftc/2023/11/preventing-harms-ai-enabled-voice-cloning" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ftc.gov">[Federal Trade Commission+2wiley.law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ftc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">preventing harms ai enabled voice cloning</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Trade CommissionPreventing the Harms of AI-enabled Voice Cloning16 Nov 2023 — The FTC is announcing the Voice Cloning Challenge t...</span></span></span>

The psychological effect is significant because voice carries emotional signals. A panicked voice, an excited voice or a reassuring voice can trigger rapid reactions before deliberate verification takes place.

Research on speech [deepfakes]({{ 'deepfakes/' | relative_url }}) has found that people are not consistently reliable at identifying synthetic voices. Even when participants know deepfakes exist, detection performance remains imperfect and is expected to become more difficult as synthesis quality improves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.07829" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Warning: Humans Cannot Reliably Detect Speech Deepfakes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Warning: Humans Cannot Reliably Detect Speech DeepfakesJanuary 19, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 19, 2023</span></span></span>

The lesson is straightforward: a familiar voice is no longer strong proof that a familiar person is actually speaking.

## Screenshots, Documents and “Evidence” Can Be Manufactured

Another trust signal that AI can imitate is documentary evidence.

Social media users often treat screenshots as proof. A screenshot appears concrete. It seems to show a message, a search result, a private conversation or an official document. Yet generative image systems can now create realistic interfaces, chat logs and document layouts.

Recent research on synthetic credibility highlights how AI can generate visual artefacts that resemble trusted forms of evidence, including realistic embedded text, official-looking formats and familiar platform designs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03348" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SynCred-Bench: Benchmarking Synthetic Credibility in AI-Generated Visual MisinformationJune 2, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 2, 2026</span></span></span>

This creates a subtle problem. People may spend time checking whether a claim sounds plausible while spending little time checking whether the supposed evidence is genuine.

A screenshot should therefore be treated as a lead, not as verification. The key question is whether the original source can be independently located.

## How to Check Voices, Screenshots and Creator Identity

The most effective defence is not learning to spot every deepfake. Experts increasingly argue that the real challenge is verifying identity and evidence rather than relying on intuition about whether something “looks fake”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04951" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

### For voices

If a voice message or call requests money, urgent action or sensitive information:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Pause before responding.
* Contact the person through a known channel rather than replying directly.
* Verify details independently.

## The Key Shift: Trust the Verification Process, Not the Performance

In the past, many trust cues were expensive to fake. Producing a convincing voice recording, professional video, polished article or community-specific message required substantial time and skill. Generative AI dramatically lowers those costs.

That does not mean trust is impossible. It means that the visible performance of trustworthiness is becoming easier to manufacture.

A convincing voice, relatable personality, local reference or professional presentation may still be genuine. The problem is that these signals are no longer reliable on their own. In an environment shaped by AI-generated media, the safest habit is to separate the messenger from the evidence and to verify both independently. Trust cues remain useful, but they should start an investigation rather than end it.
